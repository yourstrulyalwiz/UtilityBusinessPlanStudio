1.5         Sanitation 2

Currently, Tagbilaran City is served by various wastewater management providers to maintain
environmental standards and public health. These providers are responsible for ensuring that
wastewater from residential, commercial, and industrial sources is appropriately treated and
disposed of in compliance with national and local regulations. Industrial and commercial entities,
such as large resort hotels discharging 100 cubic meters (cu.m.) per day, are required to install their
treatment facilities for their wastewater.

In terms of septage management, there are planned facilities to address the sanitation needs in
Tagbilaran City. A septage treatment plant (SpTP) in Tagbilaran City, located near the bus terminal,
is currently being developed. It has a capacity of 24 cu.m./day. It is a pilot SpTP under a Japan
International Cooperation Agency (JICA) project. Another proposed SpTP has a capacity of 25-100
cu.m./day to be located at Brgy. Tiptip. It is intended to serve Tagbilaran City only.

2.2       Asset Management Plan

TCWS needed to have a thorough understanding of the water system’s infrastructure and its
capabilities or an asset management plan. A simple asset management plan answers five core
questions: What do we own? What service do we need to deliver? What are our most important
assets? What do we need to do to keep them operating? How do we pay for it?

To guide the exercise, the study team introduced four primary reasons why assets fail: mortality,
capacity, level of service, and financial efficiency (U.S. EPA, 2016).

      ■   Mortality occurs when an asset physically fails or can no longer perform its intended
          function.

      ■   Capacity means the asset remains operational but cannot meet the required output.

      ■   Level of service refers to an asset that works but fails to deliver the expected service
          quality.

      ■   Financial efficiency arises when operating costs become uneconomical.

The study team also presented the concept of an asset registry, the foundation of any asset
management plan. During the exercise, the waterworks team learned how to create a basic registry,
apply proper tagging, and record essential data for each asset. Table 8 below shows a sample
registry, using the actual assets of TCWS including pertinent data: location, capacity, condition and

The concept of criticality was also introduced to the Tagbilaran Waterworks personnel. Criticality
is defined as the measure of risk associated with an asset, calculated as the Probability of Failure
(PoF) × Consequence of Failure (CoF) (Betgeri, Vatyala & Matthews, 2024). For effective planning, the
waterworks must identify the assets that are most likely to fail; assess the impact of such failures;
and determine the optimal timing for replacement or procurement of critical spares.

Understanding the principles of asset inventory, criticality, and asset management is essential for
maximizing financial resources while minimizing downtime. By applying criticality analysis, projects
and asset renewals can be prioritized to ensure the efficient use of limited funds and personnel
(Moss & Woodhouse, 1999).

The matrix in Table 9 below serves as the basis for determining asset criticality ratings. Each asset
was scored on two dimensions: PoF and CoF, using a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 = lowest and 5 = highest.
While other scoring systems can be applied, the 1–5 scale is widely used in industry. The maximum
criticality score is 25 (PoF × CoF), with higher scores indicating greater risk.

2.3       SWOT (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats) Analysis

The study team then facilitated a SWOT Analysis exercise. TCWS was given examples and then
asked to look at its strengths and weaknesses as a water service provider. This includes evaluating
where it was good or weak at; how reliable or unreliable their water system is; how well maintained
or not are their assets; how their customers see them and areas for improvement. The WSP was
then asked to evaluate the external environment and identify opportunities and threats. For
opportunities, the following questions were raised to trigger the discussion: Which unserved areas
can we expand to? Where are the high growth development areas in the city? How can we partner with
the province? How can we maximize existing septage facilities? What technology solutions can we
adopt to address some of our existing issues? For threats, the waterworks was asked the following:
How stable or volatile is our electricity cost? What is the risk of our customers being unable to pay the
water tariff? What is the threat from so many other water providers in the city? Are our water sources
threatened by land-based pollution and/or salt water intrusion?

2.4.1       Action Plan

Following the first three steps and being aware of their weaknesses as a utility, the fourth step was
to create an action plan to achieve their goals. To facilitate the exercise, the study team identified

Unconstrained water demand refers to the volume of water required to serve the target population
at the desired service level, assuming no limitations. This estimate accounts for all factors
influencing consumption, such as population growth, economic activity, legislation, and policy
implementation. It does not, however, consider constraints like pricing, willingness or ability to pay,
or even technical limitations (e.g., network capacity or insufficient water sources).

represents the average daily water requirement within the project area. This involved projecting
water demand for residential households, commercial establishments, and institutional users, while

## D. Corrections to the preliminary business plan

1. Connections: use 6,461 (Dec 2024) and 6,314 (Dec 2025). The plan's 6,084 (6,017/34/33) comes from a per-barangay breakdown that is repeated unchanged across 2022–2025 records.
2. "Annual water sales" (PhP 22,887,685.65 in 2024; PhP 12,036,951.99 in 2025) are **collections**; the 2025 figure covers **Jan–Jul**, not Jan–Jun. Billings are not recorded, so collection efficiency cannot be calculated.
3. "Currently charging PhP 6.20/m³" refers only to the minimum charge (PhP 62 for 10 m³). The actual tariff is the 2016 block tariff above; the "average tariff 13.76" in Table 5 is the 2023 cost-recovery tariff, not the actual average.
4. NRW: annual NRW was 46.3% (2023) and 39.9% (2024). The 31% for H1 2025 is provisional: several 2025 production readings repeat the previous month's value.
5. Production is declining (9.69 → 8.35 → 7.66 MLD); several units report zero output in Sep–Dec 2024.
6. CAPEX in the plan (PhP 255.4 M) is lower than in the financial model (PhP 579.2 M for 2026–2029).
7. The tariff calculation in the financial model divides the revenue requirement by a volume (15.25 M m³ in 2030) that includes 8.56 M m³ from private providers; on bulk water volume alone (6.69 M m³) the required average tariff would be about PhP 50/m³.
8. The financial model has two OPEX versions (power PhP 13.13/m³ vs PhP 10.3/m³), a zero chemical cost despite a PhP 0.5/m³ assumption, and Year 5 labelled 2034 (plan: 2035).
9. Collections per m³ billed (PhP 12.49 in 2024) are high compared with a typical residential bill under the 2016 tariff (about PhP 6.8/m³ at 23 m³/month); this needs reconciliation (arrears, penalties, connection fees or under-recorded volume).
